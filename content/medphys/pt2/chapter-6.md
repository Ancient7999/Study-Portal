## 1. Overview & learning map

### What this chapter covers

Chapter 6 uses the Chapter 5 lens rules for **thin lenses, power and sign rules**, covered in the Periodic Test 2 notes, to explain real instruments: the **simple magnifier**, the **compound microscope** and the **human eye**. It then covers the **vision defects** myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism and presbyopia, and how lenses correct them, and finishes with **lasers**: what they are, their properties, their parts and their medical uses. It starts with **combination of lenses**, the last part of Chapter 5, because a microscope is two lenses working together.

### Topics map

| Topic | Core idea |
|-------|-----------|
| Combination of lenses | Two lenses act as one: 1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂ − r/(f₁f₂) |
| Simple magnifier | Convex lens, object inside F → virtual, erect, magnified image; M = 25 cm / f |

**Study tip:** for every instrument, learn **where the object sits**, inside F or just outside F, and the **three words** for the image: real/virtual, erect/inverted, magnified/diminished.

## 2. Combination of lenses

Two lenses of focal length **f₁** and **f₂** placed a distance **r** apart act like **one** lens of focal length **f**:

```
1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂ − r / (f₁ × f₂)
Power:  P = P₁ + P₂ − r P₁ P₂
```

If the two lenses **touch**, r = 0 and the last term disappears:

```
1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂          P = P₁ + P₂
```

```example
Given two thin lenses in contact, P₁ = +2 D and P₂ = +3 D
P = 2 + 3 = +5 D
f = 1 / P = 1 / 5 = 0.2 m = 20 cm
```

**Remember from Chapter 5:** power P = 1/f with f in **metres**, unit **dioptre (D)**. A convex lens has **+** power and a concave lens **−** power. These rules are covered in the Periodic Test 2 notes, Chapter 5 · Ray optics.

### Worked examples

**Two convex lenses, f₁ = 15 cm and f₂ = 25 cm, placed 5 cm apart**

```
1/f = 1/15 + 1/25 − 5 / (15 × 25)
1/f = 25/375 + 15/375 − 5/375 = 35/375
f = 375 / 35 ≈ 10.7 cm
```

**Two lenses of power −1.75 D and +2.25 D in contact**

```
P = P₁ + P₂ = −1.75 + 2.25 = +0.5 D
f = 1 / P = 1 / 0.5 = 2 m   ← a converging combination
```

## 3. The simple magnifier

- A **simple magnifier**, or **magnifying glass**, is a **convex lens of short focal length**, used to see a **magnified image of a small object**.
- **Principle:** when a small object is placed **between the optical centre and the focus (F)** of a convex lens, a **virtual, erect and magnified** image forms on the **same side** of the lens as the object.
- **Working:** the lens is held close to the eye and the object distance is adjusted until the image forms at the **least distance of distinct vision**, **N = 25 cm**, from the eye.

### Angular magnification

```
M = θ / θ₀ = 25 cm / f
θ₀ = angle the object makes at the eye without the lens, with the object at 25 cm
θ  = angle the image makes at the eye with the lens
f  = focal length in cm
```

**How M = 25 cm / f is made:** without the lens, the closest you can hold the object is 25 cm, so it looks small: it fills only a small angle θ₀. With the lens you can bring it to about one focal length from the lens, so it fills a bigger angle θ. The ratio of the angles is about 25 cm / f. A **shorter focal length** gives a **bigger magnification**.

| Object position for a convex lens | Image |
|---|---|
| Between the lens and F: magnifier | Virtual, erect, magnified, same side |
| Exactly at F | No image: the rays come out parallel |
| Between F and 2F | Real, inverted, magnified |
| Beyond 2F | Real, inverted, diminished |

### Worked examples

**Example: f = 10 cm**

```
M = 25 cm / f = 25 / 10 = 2.5
```

**Magnifier with f = 5 cm**, image at 25 cm

```
M = 25 / 5 = 5   ← the image looks 5 times bigger
```

## 4. The compound microscope

- A **compound microscope** gives **greater magnification** by **combining two convex lenses**.
- **Objective lens:** very **short focal length**, **fₒ < 1 cm**.
- **Eyepiece:** focal length **fₑ of a few centimetres**, greater than fₒ.
- The two lenses are separated by a distance **L**, the tube length, that is **much greater than either fₒ or fₑ**.

### How the image is made

1. The **object** is placed **just outside the focal point of the objective**, between F and 2F.
2. The objective forms a **real, inverted, large image I₁**. I₁ falls **at or just inside the focal point of the eyepiece**.
3. The **eyepiece works as a simple magnifier** on I₁ and makes the final image **I₂**: **virtual and enlarged**.
4. The **final image stays inverted** relative to the original object, because the eyepiece does not turn it back.

## 5. The human eye

- The **human eye resembles a camera**: it has an **adjustable lens**, an **iris** that acts as the aperture, and a **retina** that acts as the film or sensor.
- Light passes through the **cornea**, the clear front surface; then the **aqueous humor**, the clear liquid behind the cornea; then the **pupil**, an opening in the iris that works as a variable aperture; and then the **crystalline lens**.
- The **refractive index of the lens material is 1.437**. For comparison, water is 1.33 and air is 1.00.
- **Most of the refraction happens at the cornea.** The lens makes the **small adjustments** to focus at different distances. This is called accommodation.
- The **vitreous humor** is the gel that fills the eyeball behind the lens.
- The image on the **retina** is **real, inverted and diminished**; the brain turns it the right way up.

## 6. Near point and far point

- **Near point:** the **closest** distance at which the eye can focus clearly. It has an **average value of 25 cm** for an adult; at **age 10 it is about 18 cm**. It **increases with age** as the lens becomes less flexible. This 25 cm is the **least distance of distinct vision, N**, used in the magnifier formula.
- **Far point:** the **greatest** distance at which the **relaxed** eye can focus. For normal vision it is taken as **infinity**.

## 7. Vision defects and their correction

There are **4 types of vision defects**, depending on the eye lens and the cornea:
1. **Nearsightedness**, or myopia
2. **Farsightedness**, or hyperopia
3. **Astigmatism**
4. **Presbyopia**, old-age vision

### Myopia: nearsightedness

- You see **near objects clearly**, but **far objects are blurry**.
- Light is bent too strongly, so images of distant objects form **in front of the retina**.
- **Cause:** the **focal length of the eye lens decreases**, giving too much optical power, and/or the **eye is too long**.
- **Correction:** a **diverging, concave lens**, a "**minus power**" lens. It spreads the rays a little before they enter the eye, so the image moves back onto the retina.

### Hyperopia: farsightedness

- You see **far objects clearly**, but have **difficulty with near objects**: the **near point is too far away**, much farther than 25 cm.
- Images of near objects would form **behind the retina**.

- **Correction:** a **converging, convex lens**, a "**plus power**" lens. It adds power, so the image moves forward onto the retina.

### Summary table

| | Myopia | Hyperopia |
|---|---|---|
| Sees clearly | Near objects | Far objects |
| Blurry | Far objects | Near objects |
| Image forms | In front of the retina | Behind the retina |
| Cause | Focal length too short, so too much power, or eye too long | Focal length too long, so too little power, or eye too short |
| Correction | Diverging, concave, minus lens | Converging, convex, plus lens |

**Astigmatism and presbyopia:** in **astigmatism** the cornea or lens is unevenly curved, so lines in one direction are blurred; it is corrected with a **cylindrical lens**. **Presbyopia** is old-age vision: the lens stiffens, the near point moves away, and convex reading glasses help.

## 8. Lasers

**LASER = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.** It is a **strong, highly monochromatic, collimated, directional and coherent** beam of light.

### Properties of laser light

| Property | Meaning | Ordinary light |
|---|---|---|
| **Highly monochromatic** | One wavelength, so one colour | White light is a mix of many wavelengths |
| **Highly directional** | Does not spread or diverge as it travels | Spreads in many directions, like the sun, a bulb or a candle |
| **Highly coherent** | Waves are **in phase in space and time** | Incoherent |
| **Highly bright / intense** | Many photons in a small area; brightness = power per area per solid angle, so even a moderate-power laser is extremely bright because it is so directional | Much less bright |

**Hazard:** these properties make laser light **more hazardous than ordinary light**: it can **deposit a great deal of energy within a very small area**. Always protect the eyes.

### The three parts of a laser

1. **Active medium:** the material that **amplifies light by stimulated emission**, e.g. a ruby crystal.
2. **Energy source:** **pumps energy** into the active medium, e.g. a flash lamp.
3. **Reflecting cavity:** usually **two mirrors** that let the light **bounce back and forth** so it is amplified; one mirror lets part of the beam out.

Example: the **solid-state ruby laser**.

## 9. Applications of lasers

1. **Micro-surgery:** a laser beam can **destroy harmful tissue without seriously affecting the nearby regions**. It also **seals small blood vessels as it cuts**: it cauterizes, so there is little bleeding: the "**bloodless knife**".
2. **Skin diseases** are treated with lasers.
3. **Low-power semiconductor lasers** are used in **CD players, laser printers and laser copiers**.
4. A **narrow red laser beam** reads **bar codes** in supermarkets.
5. Lasers can **separate the isotopes** of an element.
6. In medical research lasers make **three-dimensional images** of objects: **holography**.
7. **Ophthalmology**, eye medicine: two uses, below.

- **Photocoagulation of the retina:** the laser **heats a blood vessel**, the **blood coagulates and blocks the vessel**. It is used, for example, in diabetic retinopathy.
- **LASIK**, **Laser-Assisted In Situ Keratomileusis**, **reshapes the cornea** to correct **myopia and hyperopia**.

## 11. Formula sheet — Chapter 6

```
Lenses r apart:     1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂ − r / (f₁ f₂)
                    P = P₁ + P₂ − r P₁ P₂
Lenses in contact:  1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂,   P = P₁ + P₂
Power:              P = 1 / f   ← f in m, P in dioptres
Magnifier:          M = θ / θ₀ = 25 cm / f
Least distance of distinct vision:  N = 25 cm
```

### Key numbers at a glance

- Near point: **25 cm** for an adult, **18 cm** at age 10; far point: **infinity**  
- Microscope objective: **fₒ < 1 cm**; eyepiece: **a few cm**  
- Refractive index of the eye lens: **1.437**  
- Magnifier with f = 5 cm: **M = 5**; with f = 10 cm: **M = 2.5**  

### Key Points to Remember
- A simple magnifier needs the object **inside F**; the image is **virtual, erect and magnified**.  
- In a compound microscope the object is **just outside Fₒ**. The objective makes a **real, inverted, large** image I₁, and the final image is **virtual, enlarged and inverted**.  
- The image on the retina is **real, inverted and diminished**. The **cornea** does most of the refraction.  
- **Myopia**: near objects are clear; corrected with a **minus, concave** lens. **Hyperopia**: far objects are clear; corrected with a **plus, convex** lens.  
- Laser light is **monochromatic, directional, coherent and intense**. A laser has three parts: an **active medium**, an **energy source** and a **reflecting cavity**.  

### Medical Applications
- **LASIK** reshapes the **cornea** to correct myopia and hyperopia.  
- **Photocoagulation**: laser heat clots the blood and blocks the vessel.  
- Used as a **bloodless knife**, a laser cuts tissue and seals small vessels at the same time.  
- The near point moving farther away with age is **presbyopia**.
