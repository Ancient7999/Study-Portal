## 2. Waves and oscillations

### What a wave is

A **wave** is a **disturbance that propagates**, or moves, from the place where it was created.

**Propagation** of a wave simply means the **travel of the wave** through space or through a medium.

There are **three types of waves**:
1. **Mechanical waves** **need a medium**, a material such as air, water or a solid, to travel through, e.g. sound, water waves.
2. **Electromagnetic waves** **need no medium**: they can travel through vacuum, at the **speed of light**, 3 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum, e.g. light, X-rays.
3. **Matter waves** are waves linked to moving particles such as electrons.

### Sound waves

- Sound waves are **longitudinal mechanical waves** produced by **vibrating objects**.
- They can travel through **solids, liquids or gases (air)**. **Sound does not travel through vacuum.**
- A vibrating object, such as a **tuning fork**, disturbs the surrounding air, and the disturbance is passed on from air molecule to air molecule. This moving disturbance is a **sound wave**.
- A tuning fork is made to vibrate by striking it with a **rubber hammer**.
- As the medium is pushed back and forth, it forms a repeating pattern of **compressions** and **rarefactions** that travels outward. Compressions are squeezed, higher-pressure zones; rarefactions are stretched, lower-pressure zones.

### Longitudinal vs transverse

- **Longitudinal wave**, such as sound: the particles of the medium vibrate back and forth **in the direction the wave travels**. This makes **compressions**, where particles are bunched, and **rarefactions**, where they are spread out. Rarefactions are also called expansions.
- **Transverse wave**, such as visible light or **water waves**: the particles vibrate **at right angles** to the direction the wave travels. This makes **crests** and **troughs**.

> **Small note:** waves on a water surface are really a **mix** of transverse and longitudinal motion, because the water particles move in small circles, but they are a common simple example of a transverse wave.

### Frequency and pitch

- The **frequency of the vibrating source is the frequency of the sound**.
- **Higher frequency → higher pitch**, as in a police siren. **Lower frequency → lower pitch.**
- Clinical use: a **tuning fork** is used to test a patient's **hearing** and **vibration sense**, for example in nerve damage.

### Sine wave vs sawtooth wave

These two wave shapes are **not the same**:
- A **sine wave** is a **smooth**, rounded wave that rises and falls in the same gentle way. This is the usual wave shape drawn for sound and light.
- A **sawtooth wave** **rises in a straight line**, like a steady ramp, and then **drops sharply** back down, like the teeth of a saw.

| | Sine wave | Sawtooth wave |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Smooth, rounded curve | Straight ramps with sudden drops |
| Rising part | Gentle and curved | Steady straight-line rise |
| Falling part | Gentle and curved, the same as the rise | Sudden, almost vertical drop |
| Edges | No sharp corners | Sharp corner at each drop |
| Harmonics | Only one frequency: a pure tone | The main frequency plus many harmonics |
| Sound | Pure, soft tone | Bright, buzzy, harsh tone |

## 3. Frequency, wavelength & wave velocity

### Definitions

- **Crest:** the **highest point** of a wave.
- **Trough:** the **lowest point** of a wave.
- **Wavelength (λ):** the length of **one cycle**, i.e. the distance between two neighbouring identical points of a wave, such as crest to crest or trough to trough.
- **Amplitude (A):** the height of the wave above the **rest position**, the undisturbed state.
- **Frequency (f):** how often a vibration, or cycle, happens: the **number of cycles per unit time**, f = cycles / time. SI unit: **hertz (Hz)**; **1 Hz = 1 vibration/s = 1 cycle/s = 1/s = s⁻¹**.
- **Time period (T):** the time to complete **one** vibration, or cycle. It is the **inverse of frequency**.
- **Wave velocity (v):** the speed at which the disturbance moves. It is also called the **propagation velocity** or propagation speed.

```
Frequency:     f = number of cycles / time   ← unit 1/s = s⁻¹ = Hz
Time period:   T = 1 / f
Wave speed:    v = λ × f
At a fixed speed:   f ∝ 1 / λ
Intensity:     I ∝ A²   ← double the amplitude → 4 × the intensity
```

**Intensity and amplitude:** the **intensity** of a wave, the energy it carries, is proportional to the **square of its amplitude**. A louder sound has a bigger amplitude.

**How v = λf is made:** in one period T the wave moves forward by one wavelength λ. Speed = distance ÷ time = λ / T, and 1/T = f, so v = λ × f. If the speed stays the same, a **higher frequency** means a **shorter wavelength** (f ∝ 1/λ).

## 4. Speed of sound

```
v = √(B / ρ)
B = bulk modulus of the medium = how hard it is to compress,  ρ = density
```

**How v = √(B/ρ) is used:** a **stiffer** medium, with a bigger B, passes the push on faster; a **heavier** medium, with a bigger ρ, responds more slowly. Solids are very stiff, so sound is fastest in solids, slower in liquids, and slowest in gases.

- Sound waves **do not travel through vacuum**.
- Sound waves travel **much slower than light** and other electromagnetic waves.
- The **bulk modulus B** of the medium, its resistance to being squeezed, affects the speed of sound: v = √(B/ρ).
- The speed of sound **depends on the medium**: in **air at 20 °C** it is **343 m/s**; in **water at 20 °C** it is **1482 m/s**; in **steel** it is **5941 m/s**.

> **Remember:** the speed of sound in **air at 20 °C = 343 m/s**. Most problems use this number.

### The speed of sound in different media

| Medium | Speed (m/s) |
|---|---|
| Air (0 °C) | 331 |
| Air (20 °C) | 343 |
| Helium | 965 |
| Hydrogen | 1284 |
| Water (0 °C) | 1402 |
| Water (20 °C) | 1482 |
| Seawater | 1522 |

### Practice example

**Speed of sound in water from v = √(B/ρ)**  
For water, B = 2.2 × 10⁹ Pa and ρ = 1000 kg/m³.

```
v = √(2.2 × 10⁹ / 1000) = √(2.2 × 10⁶) ≈ 1483 m/s
```

This matches the table value for water at 20 °C (1482 m/s).

## 6. Ultrasound imaging

- **Ultrasound imaging** is a **non-invasive** technique used to see the **structure, movement and function** of the body's **organs and blood vessels**.
- Image acquisition is **easy and fast**, using sound of frequency **2 MHz to 15 MHz**.
- It is **non-ionizing** radiation, **unlike X-ray / CT**, so it is safe in pregnancy.

| 2.5 MHz | Deep abdomen, obstetric and gynecological imaging |
| 3.5 MHz | General abdomen, obstetric and gynecological imaging |

### Worked examples

**Wavelength of a 512 Hz tuning fork in air**  
The speed of sound in air is v = 343 m/s.

```
v = λ × f   →   λ = v / f
λ = 343 / 512 ≈ 0.67 m
```

**Ultrasonic scanner in tissue**  
The scanner works at f = 3.8 MHz, and sound travels through tissue at v = 1.5 km/s.

```
f = 3.8 MHz = 3.8 × 10⁶ Hz
v = 1.5 km/s = 1500 m/s
λ = v / f = 1500 / (3.8 × 10⁶) ≈ 3.95 × 10⁻⁴ m ≈ 0.39 mm
```

**Period of the tuning fork**

```
T = 1 / f = 1 / 512 ≈ 1.95 × 10⁻³ s ≈ 1.95 ms
```

## 7. Doppler effect

### What it is

The **Doppler effect** is the **change in the frequency**, an increase or a decrease, of sound, light or other waves when the **source and the observer move towards or away from each other**. It was described in **1842** by the Austrian physicist **Christian Doppler**.

- A **police car or ambulance approaching** you: the siren sounds **higher pitched**: the frequency you hear is **increased**.
- As it **goes away**: the siren sounds **lower pitched**: the frequency **decreases**.

### Doppler shift formula

If the detector, which is the observer, the source, or both are moving, the **emitted frequency f** and the **detected frequency f′** are related by:

```
f′ = f × (v ± v_D) / (v ± v_S)
v = speed of sound in air,  v_S = speed of the source,  v_D = speed of the detector, the observer
```

**How to pick the signs:** moving **towards** each other must make f′ **bigger**; moving **apart** must make f′ **smaller**.
- Observer moving **towards** the source → **+ v_D** on top; **away** → **− v_D**.
- Source moving **towards** the observer → **− v_S** on the bottom; **away** → **+ v_S**.

**Source and detector moving towards each other**, with both signs chosen to raise f′:

```
f′ = f × (v + v_D) / (v − v_S)
```

All the cases:

| Situation | Top: detector | Bottom: source | Effect on f′ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Detector moves **towards** the source | v **+** v_D | | f′ higher |
| Detector moves **away** from the source | v **−** v_D | | f′ lower |
| Source moves **towards** the detector | | v **−** v_S | f′ higher |
| Source moves **away** from the detector | | v **+** v_S | f′ lower |
| Detector or source **at rest** | v, since v_D = 0 | v, since v_S = 0 | no change from that body |

In this example the **source** is the moving **ambulance**, with speed v_S, and the **detector** is the **person standing** on the street, at rest, so v_D = 0.

Observer at rest, so v_D = 0:

```
A) Source moving TOWARDS the observer:   f′ = f × v / (v − v_S)    → f′ > f
B) Source moving AWAY from the observer:  f′ = f × v / (v + v_S)    → f′ < f
```

### Medical link

**Doppler ultrasound** uses this effect: ultrasound reflected from **moving red blood cells** comes back with a shifted frequency, which gives the **speed and direction of blood flow**. Chapter 3 lists its uses: carotid arteries, DVT, fetal circulation and heart valves.

### Practice example

An ambulance siren has f = 700 Hz and moves at v_S = 30 m/s. The speed of sound is v = 343 m/s, and the observer is at rest.

```
Approaching:  f′ = 700 × 343 / (343 − 30) = 700 × 343 / 313 ≈ 767 Hz
Going away:   f′ = 700 × 343 / (343 + 30) = 700 × 343 / 373 ≈ 644 Hz
```

Higher pitch as it comes, lower pitch as it leaves.

## 8. Shock waves & Mach number

### Shock waves

- If a **source of sound moves faster than the speed of sound** (vₛ > v), **shock waves** are produced.
- The wave fronts **lag behind** the source in a **cone-shaped region** with the source at the **tip**, the vertex.
- The edge of the cone is a **supersonic wave front** with an unusually **large amplitude**: the **shock wave**.
- When a shock wave reaches an observer, a **sonic boom** is heard. It comes from the **piling up of waves**.

### Mach number

```
Ma = vₛ / v
vₛ = speed of the source,  v = speed of sound
```

**How Ma is made:** it is just "how many times faster than sound". A plane at **Mach 2.3** flies at **2.3 times the speed of sound** in the air it flies through, so it makes shock waves.

| Mach number | Name |
|---|---|
| Ma < 1 | Subsonic: slower than sound |
| Ma ≈ 1 | Transonic |
| Ma > 1 | Supersonic: shock waves form |

> **Note:** subsonic means **Ma < 1**. Ma = 0 would mean the source is not moving at all.

- Shock waves from a supersonic jet can become **visible**: the sudden **drop in air pressure** and temperature in them makes **water vapour in the air condense**, forming a **fog**. This is the cone-shaped **condensation cloud** seen in photos of jets.

### Practice example

Speed of a plane at Mach 2.3 in air at 20 °C:

```
vₛ = Ma × v = 2.3 × 343 ≈ 789 m/s
```

### Shock waves in medicine: breaking kidney stones with ESWL

**ESWL** stands for **extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy**: *extracorporeal* = **outside the body**, *lithotripsy* = **stone crushing**.

- A machine **outside the body** makes strong **shock waves**: short, high-pressure pulses.
- They pass into the body through a **water cushion or gel**, because sound passes well through water and soft tissue.
- The waves are **focused** onto the **kidney stone**, which is found with **X-ray or ultrasound** imaging.
- Many shocks **break the stone into small pieces**, which then **leave the body in the urine**.
- It is **non-invasive**: no cut in the skin is needed.

## 9. The zeroth law of thermodynamics

Two bodies are in **thermal equilibrium** if they are at the **same temperature** throughout, so **no heat flows** from one to the other.

- The thick border is an **insulating box**: no heat gets in or out.
- **S** is an **insulating wall**: **this wall blocks heat transfer between the two chambers**, so A and B cannot exchange heat while S is there.
- **T** is a **thermoscope**, a simple thermometer. Its bottom face, the coloured strip, is **conducting**: a conducting wall **lets heat pass**, so T and the body it touches can reach the **same temperature**.
- **(a)** T touches **A** and settles at a reading. **(b)** T touches **B** and shows the **same reading**. So A and B are each in equilibrium with T.
- **(c)** The wall is removed and **A touches B**: **nothing changes**, no heat flows, because A and B were already at the same temperature. This is the zeroth law.

**Why it matters:** **thermometers work using thermal equilibrium**. The thermometer, body T, reaches the same temperature as the patient, so its reading is the patient's temperature.

## 10. Temperature scales: Kelvin, Celsius & Fahrenheit

### Triple point of water

The **triple point of water**, or of any other substance, is the **single** temperature and pressure at which **solid ice, liquid water and water vapour** all **coexist in equilibrium**. For water:

```
Triple point of water:  273.16 K  =  0.01 °C
at a pressure of about 611.657 Pa   ← ≈ 0.6 kPa ≈ 0.006 atm
```

**Which pressure?** Boiling and freezing points are given at **standard atmospheric pressure**, 1 atm = **101 325 Pa**. So "at a particular pressure" is true for them: water boils at 100 °C and freezes at 0 °C **at 1 atm**. The triple point is **different**: it happens only at a **much lower pressure**, about **611.657 Pa**, roughly 1/166 of 1 atm. That is why the triple point (0.01 °C) is not the same as the normal freezing point (0 °C).

### Fixed points to remember

| Point | Kelvin | Celsius | Fahrenheit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Boiling point of water** at 1 atm | **373.15 K** | **100 °C** | **212 °F** |
| **Freezing point of water** at 1 atm | **273.15 K** | **0 °C** | **32 °F** |
| Triple point of water at ≈ 611.657 Pa | 273.16 K | 0.01 °C | 32.02 °F |
| **Absolute zero** | **0 K** | **−273.15 °C** | **−459.67 °F** |

**Degree sign:** Celsius and Fahrenheit use **degrees** (**°C**, **°F**). Kelvin does **not**: write **K** with no degree sign: 300 K, not 300 °K.

### Defining the scales

```
Celsius from Kelvin:      T_C = T_K − 273.15
Fahrenheit from Celsius:  T_F = (9/5) T_C + 32
```

> **Note:** the exact conversion uses **273.15**: 0 °C = 273.15 K, not 273.16 K. **273.16 K** is the **triple point**, 0.01 °C. In problems this is usually rounded to **273**.

**Near absolute zero:** temperatures near **0 K** (**−273.15 °C**, **−459.67 °F**), where **thermal motion reaches its minimum** possible value. Nothing can be colder than 0 K.

### Conversion formulas used in problems

```
Celsius to Kelvin:      T_K = T_C + 273
Kelvin to Celsius:      T_C = T_K − 273
Celsius to Fahrenheit:  T_F = (9/5) T_C + 32
Fahrenheit to Celsius:  T_C = (5/9)(T_F − 32)
```

**How T_F = (9/5)T_C + 32 is made:** water freezes at 0 °C = 32 °F and boils at 100 °C = 212 °F. So 100 Celsius degrees cover 180 Fahrenheit degrees: each °C step is **180/100 = 9/5** °F steps, and the Fahrenheit scale **starts 32 higher**. The reverse formula just undoes these two steps.

### Worked examples

**Body temperature 35 °C in Fahrenheit**

```
T_F = (9/5) × T_C + 32
T_F = (9/5) × 35 + 32 = 63 + 32 = 95 °F
```

**Convert 15 °C to Kelvin**

```
T_K = T_C + 273 = 15 + 273 = 288 K
```

### Practice examples

**Fever of 104 °F in Celsius**

```
T_C = (5/9)(T_F − 32) = (5/9)(104 − 32) = (5/9) × 72 = 40 °C
```

**300 K in Celsius**

```
T_C = T_K − 273 = 300 − 273 = 27 °C
```

## 12. Formula sheet — Chapter 4

```
Frequency: f = cycles / time              ← unit Hz = 1/s = s⁻¹
Period: T = 1 / f                         ← time for one cycle [s]
Intensity: I ∝ A²                         ← amplitude squared
Wave speed: v = λ × f                     ← f ∝ 1/λ at fixed speed
Speed of sound: v = √(B / ρ)              ← stiffer → faster; denser → slower
Air 20 °C: 343 m/s · water: 1482 m/s · steel: 5941 m/s
Ranges: infrasound < 20 Hz < audible < 20 000 Hz < ultrasound
Medical ultrasound: 2–15 MHz
Doppler: f′ = f (v ± v_D) / (v ± v_S)     ← towards → f′ up; away → f′ down
Towards each other: f′ = f (v + v_D) / (v − v_S)
Source towards, observer still: f′ = f v / (v − v_S)
Source away, observer still:    f′ = f v / (v + v_S)
Mach number: Ma = vₛ / v                  ← > 1 supersonic → shock waves
Kelvin ↔ Celsius: T_K = T_C + 273, exactly 273.15
Celsius → Fahrenheit: T_F = (9/5) T_C + 32
Fahrenheit → Celsius: T_C = (5/9)(T_F − 32)
Triple point of water: 273.16 K = 0.01 °C
Absolute zero: 0 K = −273.15 °C = −459.67 °F
```

### Key numbers at a glance

- Speed of sound in air (20 °C): **343 m/s**  
- 1 Hz = 1/s = **s⁻¹**; intensity ∝ **A²**  
- Human hearing: **20 Hz – 20 000 Hz**  
- Medical ultrasound: **2 – 15 MHz**  
- Body temperature: **37 °C = 98.6 °F**  
- −40 °C = **−40 °F**: the scales meet  
- Water boils at **100 °C = 212 °F = 373.15 K** and freezes at **0 °C = 32 °F = 273.15 K** at 1 atm = 101 325 Pa  
- Triple point: **273.16 K = 0.01 °C** at ≈ **611.657 Pa**; absolute zero: **0 K = −273.15 °C = −459.67 °F**  

### Key Points to Remember
- Sound is a **longitudinal mechanical** wave, so it needs a medium and cannot travel through a vacuum.  
- **v = λf**: at the same speed, a higher frequency means a shorter wavelength.  
- Pitch depends on **frequency**; loudness depends on **amplitude**.  
- Doppler effect: a source moving towards the observer sounds higher; a source moving away sounds lower.  
- A source faster than sound, Ma > 1, produces a **cone** of shock waves and a **sonic boom**.  
- Bodies at the same temperature exchange **no heat**, by the zeroth law. An insulating wall blocks heat; a conducting wall lets it through.  
- Kelvin is written **without a degree sign**, K; °C and °F use one.  
- °F = **9/5 × °C + 32**; K = **°C + 273**.  

### Medical Applications
- Ultrasound is non-ionizing, **unlike X-ray / CT**.  
- Deep organs are imaged at **low** MHz; superficial organs at **high** MHz.  
- Doppler ultrasound measures the **speed and direction** of blood flow.  
- ESWL uses shock waves made **outside the body** to break **kidney stones**.  
- A thermometer works because of the **zeroth law** of thermal equilibrium.
